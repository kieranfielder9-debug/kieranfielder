The brief

You have not given me a problem yet, so I wrote this one as an example, in the template I asked you to use. It is invented. Nobody has confirmed the problem is real.

Who has it
A freelance designer with 5 to 15 quotes out at any time.
What goes wrong
They send a quote, mean to chase it, forget, and the lead goes cold.
What they do today
Memory, starred emails, a sticky note.
Hypothesis
I believe that if a freelancer could open one list of who to chase today, they would follow up on time, because the decision about who and when has already been made.
Proof
3 of 5 freelancers open it on 3 separate days in one week, and chase at least 80% of leads on the day they fall due.
Constraints
One weekend. Web. No budget.
1

Mechanism

What does the app actually do?

When a freelancer logs a lead with a follow-up date, the app puts that lead on a Today list when the date arrives, so they chase the right person without having to remember.

PM call

The hypothesis says “follow up on time”. That is an outcome, and you cannot build an outcome. I turned it into one trigger (a date arrives) and one response (a list). I rejected email reminders here. A reminder is a second mechanism, and if we built both we would not know which one worked.

Your turn

Fill in the sentence for any app you use daily: “When [user] does [action], the app does [thing], so they get [outcome].” If you need the word “and” twice, you have found two mechanisms.

2

Scope

What is the smallest thing that tests the hypothesis?

Must have

  • Add a lead: name, what it is about, date to chase
  • Today list: due and overdue leads
  • “Chased” button that sets the next date
  • Mark a lead won or lost
  • A count of chases done on time

Later

  • Login and accounts
  • Email or phone reminders
  • Editing a lead
  • Notes and history per lead
  • Hosting it online

Never, for this test

  • Sales pipeline stages
  • Reading your inbox
  • Teams and sharing
  • Invoicing
PM call

I asked one question of every feature: if I remove this, can the hypothesis still be tested? Login failed that test, so it went to Later, even though every real app has one. The on-time count passed it, so it stayed, even though no user would ask for it. Scope serves the test first and the user's wish list second.

Your turn

Before reading further, pick one item from Later that you would have fought to keep. Write one sentence on why. Then check whether your reason is about testing the hypothesis or about the app feeling finished.

3

User journey

What does one person do, start to finish?
  1. Opens Nudge and sees who to chase today.
  2. Sends a new quote, so adds the lead with a date three days out.
  3. The lead sits under Coming up. Days pass. Nothing brings them back.
  4. Opens Nudge again. The lead has moved to Chase today.
  5. Sends the follow-up email, then presses Chased. The lead moves back to Coming up with a new date.
  6. The client replies. Marks the lead won or lost, and it leaves the list.
PM call

Step 3 is the weak point of this app. The user has to come back unprompted, and the app gives them no reason to. I flagged it as the main risk and left it in. If testers do not return, we have learned the list alone is not enough, which is a real answer to the hypothesis. Hiding the gap behind notifications would have cost a day and blurred the result.

Your turn

Write the journey for your own idea as numbered steps, one action each. Then mark any step where time passes or the user leaves. Those are your risks.

4

Wireframe

What do they see at each step?

Copy this onto your whiteboard as boxes and labels. The circled numbers are the journey steps from stage 3.

PM call

Six journey steps did not become six screens. I checked which steps share the same information and found they all read the same list of leads, so one screen covers them. Fewer screens means less to build and nowhere for a first-time user to get lost. I also put the output above the input, which is the reverse of how most beginners draw it.

Your turn

Draw this screen on your whiteboard from memory, then draw what a second screen for “Edit a lead” would need. Count the new boxes. That count is roughly what the feature costs.

PM call

The app runs on the leads table alone. I added follow_ups because the brief's proof is “chased on the day they fall due”, and without a record of each chase that number cannot be counted. A database holds what the app needs to remember and what you need to measure. Beginners usually plan for the first and forget the second, then finish a test with no data.

Your turn

6

Action

What happens when they press the button?

The whole app rests on one rule: a lead is due when it is still open and its follow-up date is today or earlier. In the code, that rule is one line.

-- server.js, the core rule
SELECT * FROM leads
WHERE status = 'open' AND next_follow_up <= today
ORDER BY next_follow_up
The user doesThe app checksThe database changes
Adds a leadHas a name and a dateNew row in leads
Presses ChasedThe lead is openNew row in follow_ups, and next_follow_up moves 3 or 7 days on
Presses Won or LostThe lead is openstatus changes, so the rule stops matching it
PM call

Two decisions. First, Chased does two things in one press: it logs the chase and sets the next date. The user never picks a date by hand, so an open lead can never be left without one. Second, the tools. I chose plain JavaScript with the database that ships inside Node, so there is nothing to install and the app is three files you can read in one sitting. A framework would look more professional and teach you less at this stage.

Your turn

In server.js, find markChased. Add a “2 weeks” option. You will need to change one line there and add one button in index.html.

PM call

I set “done” before building, then checked against it. The real app passed these checks when I ran it: a lead dated yesterday or today shows under Chase today and one dated later does not; a late chase and an on-time chase are counted correctly; won and lost leads leave the list; a blank name or missing date is refused with a message. What is not done: it is not online, and no freelancer has used it. The app works. The hypothesis is still untested, and only five real people can change that.
